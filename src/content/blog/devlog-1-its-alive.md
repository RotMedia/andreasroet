Mandoline is a vibe-coding project I started to test the waters of what's possible when you code an app with AI. Being "genuinely surprised" is such an understatement — "shocked" at how well AI understood my assignment and made it reality is the more fitting expression.

In addition to the build-in-public YouTube videos, this devlog will take you with me on the journey to launch my first app. The log will grow over time. It'll also be a place to share wins and frustrations.

You know I make sample packs — not only to provide them as resources for other producers, but also for my own productions and live sets. I record long takes of the session: I jam on a setup, and when I have something dope, I hit record.

I get lost in the sound. Twist and tweak, let the sound evolve, escalate delay feedbacks, drown them in reverb and noise. That's the fun part.

What actually sucks (sorry for the strong language) is separating each hit, then going through every hand-drawn slice and deciding whether I want to keep it or not. This often takes more time than the actual recording itself, until everything's nice and tidy.

And because I know how much time and effort my level of quality needs, sometimes I don't do it at all. I let the excitement for the new samples fade and hope future-me has more patience for this kind of task. I know the feeling will rise again when I finish a recording session — and I dread it every time.

To me, AI up until just a few weeks ago was a tool to help me write better posts and make cool images. It helped with brainstorming, strategy, learning, and streamlining some of the mundane tasks that come with social media and YouTube. I was aware it can code, but I never felt like I had a real use case for it.

Then Anthropic dropped Fable, its most powerful model. I saw the amazing things people were building and thought: "You know what — let's see how good it really is!" Man, was I in for a surprise.

So I asked it about the app I had in mind. I got a bit carried away with the workflow ideas — automatic slicing, a handy export function to auto-name all the selected samples, all that. The response I got back was something like: sure Andreas, want me to just build it for you now?

And I sat there, eyes wide, head skeptically tilted, and responded with: Yes. Enter.

A couple of seconds later, Claude came back with a few well-thought-out questions. I answered them in plain language. A few more minutes passed, the screen changed, and it presented the audio slicer — fully functional.

I loaded a recording I'd been putting off processing for a few days. And it worked. Automatic slices, almost perfect. Handles to do the final tweak. A tree with all the found slices I could just navigate through with the arrow keys. Super fast and responsive. Select slices for export. Name the whole stack.

Literally minutes later, I had my samples done. That would usually take a mind-numbing hour. Now it was minutes.

So, what is Mandoline?

Mandoline is an automatic slicer and curation tool that frees up time and cuts out the annoying, tedious part of a music producer's work with samples. It detects transients (sound peaks) in long audio files and creates slices automatically. You can adjust each slice's start and end, apply fades (linear and logarithmic), and step through every detected slice with the arrow keys instead of hunting through a waveform with a mouse. Once you're happy, select the slices you want, and Mandoline handles the export and batch-naming for you — the exact hour-long chore this whole project started from, done in minutes.
