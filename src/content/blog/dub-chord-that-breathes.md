Most dub chords sound flat because they don't move. The notes are right, the reverb is long, and yet the whole thing sits there like a photograph instead of a room you can walk into.

The fix isn't more processing. It's motion — slow enough that you don't consciously hear it, fast enough that the chord never sounds the same twice.

## What's happening in the video

I start with a plain minor 7th on the Analog Four, no effects. Then three things get added, one at a time, so you can hear what each one contributes:

**A filter that never stops.** An LFO on the cutoff, set slower than the pattern length — around 1/8 of a bar per cycle. Because it doesn't line up with the beat, no two repeats sound identical.

**A delay send that isn't parallel.** The chord goes into the Empress Echosystem, but the send level itself is modulated. When the chord is quiet, the delay is louder — which means the tail speaks in the gaps instead of piling up on top.

**Drift in the tuning.** A few cents of detune per voice, moving independently. This is the part most people skip, and it's the difference between "chord" and "chord that's breathing."

## Why this works

Dub techno was built on tape and analog desks, where nothing was ever perfectly stable. The reverb tank shifted, the tape wobbled, the desk drifted with temperature. When those imperfections are removed, the sound gets cleaner and less interesting.

What's happening above isn't nostalgia for old gear. It's just reintroducing the small, continuous variation that made those records feel like a space rather than a file.

## What to try

Take a chord you already like. Don't add anything new — just make one parameter move slowly, out of sync with the pattern. Filter cutoff is the easiest place to start.

Then leave it running for two minutes and listen. If you get bored, the movement is too small.
