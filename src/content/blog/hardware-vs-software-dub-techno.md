## TL;DR

- **No, you don't need hardware** — filtering, tape-style delay, saturation, and modulation are all fully doable in modern software
- **Hardware's real edge is tactile, physical control** — not some sound quality software can no longer reach
- **Even the legends stay quiet about gear** — Basic Channel's Moritz von Oswald has never confirmed his actual studio setup in any interview

I have many, many years under my belt producing with software only. My first years, back in the late 90s, were all on Fruity Loops — which later became FL Studio. Those years were super productive and creative. Sure, as a teenager you have plenty of time to spend, but it wasn't less creative just because I only made music with a mouse.

So why is it almost universally accepted that hardware is "better"? And why didn't I hesitate for a second to build a hardware-based studio anyway? Let's unpack it.

## Why Hardware Feels Different

Hardware has one very specific advantage: knobs, faders, buttons, encoders — in short, a physical interface. It's intuitive, and you get to control several functions at once. Holding a big fat E minor chord with one hand while opening the low-pass filter with the other is a satisfying, genuinely life-changing experience. At least to me 😅.

That's one point. The second reason is more multi-faceted.

## Does Hardware Actually Sound Better?

You know how everybody says hardware sounds "better," right? That was true in the 90s and early 2000s. Today? Not so much.

What hardware does — and will keep doing — is provide a more natural connection to the sound. Setting a filter with a mouse is fine, but drawing in an automation curve feels clunky. Recording that same movement live with a synth, or even just a controller, is completely different. You can feel the sound; you don't have to guess when it's supposed to climax or drop out.

Of course hardware looks [ cool / intimidating — wasn't sure which you meant here, fill in ], but it's also, in some cases, more streamlined toward one specific goal than a general-purpose DAW.

Take the classic dub delay sound — a chord stab pushed through a tape echo, the kind of thing that made Lee "Scratch" Perry's dub productions legendary. Add a noisy old tape and an overdriven mixer preamp, and you get that grainy, lo-fi warmth a lot of us are chasing. Funnily enough, the exact gear behind Basic Channel's version of that sound has never actually been confirmed — Moritz von Oswald has stayed famously evasive about it in every interview he's given. So treat any "this is THE machine" story you hear as folklore, not fact — the mystery is kind of the point.

If you wanted to replicate that in software, it takes real understanding of how the sound actually comes together — the filtering, the tape character, the feedback path — and then figuring out how to build that within your DAW.

You don't have to go crazy and hunt down old hardware, though. Even cheap delay and reverb pedals get you very far. And that's exactly why people are drawn to hardware in the first place — the instruments and effects do a lot of the heavy lifting for you.

Luckily, there are plenty of ways to emulate what worked in the past.

## FAQ

**Do you need hardware to make dub techno?**
No. Everything that defines the dub techno sound — heavy filtering, tape-style delay, saturation, and modulation — is available in modern software. Hardware changes how you interact with those tools, not whether you can reach the sound at all.

**Does hardware actually sound better than software for dub techno?**
Not meaningfully anymore. That was more true in the 90s and early 2000s; today's plugin emulations are close enough that the difference rarely matters on a finished track. What hardware still offers is a more physical, real-time way of shaping sound.

**What's the classic dub techno delay sound people talk about?**
It traces back to tape echo units like the Roland RE-201 Space Echo, famous from Lee "Scratch" Perry's dub productions — long, decaying, slightly warped repeats. Any delay plugin that lets you add saturation and feedback filtering can get close to that character.

**What's the fastest way to get a dub techno sound in software?**
Start from a sample pack built for the style instead of a bare preset — it gives you the tonal character immediately, and you shape it from there with filtering, delay, and saturation instead of building the raw tone from scratch.
