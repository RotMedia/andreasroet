A live set that only works when everything goes right isn't a live set — it's a rehearsal you're doing in public.

This is the skeleton I build every set on. It's boring by design: the structure holds so that the improvisation has somewhere to happen.

## The layout

**Tracks 1–4: the machine.** Kick, sub, hats, percussion. These stay locked. Nothing clever happens here — this is the floor the rest stands on.

**Tracks 5–6: the voice.** Chords and the dub stab. This is where the set is actually played, live, with hands on the encoders.

**Track 7: the send.** A dedicated delay/reverb track everything can be thrown into. Having it as its own track instead of an insert means the tail keeps ringing after the source is gone.

**Track 8: the escape hatch.** One long recorded loop — a texture, a field recording, something that can carry twenty seconds on its own. When something goes wrong, this is what buys the time to fix it.

## Scenes, not patterns

The crossfader does the heavy lifting. Scene A is the reduced version — fewer elements, filters closed. Scene B is the full picture. Everything between them is playable in real time, and the transition isn't a jump between patterns but a move through a space.

This is why the set doesn't need forty patterns. Eight patterns plus scene movement covers more ground than a long pattern chain, and it stays in the hands instead of in the sequencer.

## The one rule

**Never change two things at once.** In the studio it's fine. On a dark stage at 2 a.m. with a monitor that isn't quite right, changing the pattern and the scene at the same time is how a set falls apart.

One change, listen, next change. It feels slower than it sounds to the room.

## What to try

Build the skeleton with sounds you already have — don't design anything new for it. Play through it for twenty minutes without stopping. Wherever you get stuck is where the structure needs work, not the sound.
